# Threat Model v0.3 — Provene

*What a receipt proves, what it does not, and who can break it.*
*Draft: August 31, 2026. v0.2 revised T-3 downward after review and added T-11. v0.3 removes commercial framing that did not belong in a security document, and updates §7 and §8 now that RFC 0001 exists.*

---

## 0. Why this document came before the code

Provene is a trust product. Its entire value is that a third party believes a receipt. A trust product that ships before its threat model has, in practice, decided its threat model by accident — and the decisions it made accidentally will be in the schema, which is the one thing that cannot be quietly changed later.

The specific problem it exists to address: **a receipt generated by a hook on a developer's machine is a self-attestation by the party who wants the change merged.** That is not a flaw to be patched. It follows structurally from choosing not to sit in the model's inference path, which is the same choice that makes Provene deterministic, free to run and possible to reimplement. The response is not to fix it but to scope it, state it first and loudest, and build the tiering that lets a policy demand something stronger where it matters.

---

## 1. Trust model: what a signature actually establishes

Sigstore keyless signing binds a short-lived Fulcio certificate to an OIDC identity and records the signature in Rekor. That chain establishes:

- **that a specific identity signed a specific byte string** (`alice@company.com` signed this receipt), and
- **that it was signed no later than a specific time** (the Rekor entry is timestamped and publicly logged), and
- **that the bytes have not changed since** (tamper evidence).

It establishes nothing whatsoever about whether the *contents* are true. Fulcio has no opinion on whether Claude wrote the code. It only knows Alice said so.

Everything else here depends on never blurring that distinction. **A Provene receipt is a signed claim, and its evidential weight comes from who signed it and whether they were in a position to observe what they claim.**

---

## 2. Assurance tiers

Every receipt carries an explicit tier. Policies are written against tiers. This is the single most important structural decision in the schema.

| Tier | Attester | Signed by | What it is actually good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T0 — recorded** | Local hook, unsigned | none (hash-chained to commit) | Local visibility, journaling. Never admissible at a merge gate. |
| **T1 — self-attested** | Local hook | Developer identity (Sigstore keyless or local key) | Attribution among cooperating parties. Prevents accidental omission and unattributed diffs. Forgeable by its own signer. |
| **T2 — CI-attested** | GitHub Action on a runner the developer does not control | CI workload identity (Actions OIDC) | Verification evidence. Tests, checks and diff coverage **re-observed independently** by a party with no stake in the merge. |
| **T3 — environment-attested** | Execution environment or agent vendor | Vendor/platform identity | Authorship itself. Not achievable by Provene alone today. |

**The tier is the interface.** "Require T2 for test evidence" is a policy a repository can write and a verifier can enforce, which is what makes it safe to describe T1 honestly instead of overselling it.

T3 is worth reserving now even though nothing produces it. Agent work is steadily moving into environments the developer does not control: cloud development environments, CI-run agents, hosted agent platforms. As it does, the achievable assurance ceiling rises without Provene building anything, provided the schema has somewhere to put the result. Design the field, leave it unpopulated.

## 3. Assets and adversaries

**Assets.** The integrity of the receipt–commit binding; the truth of verification claims; the completeness of coverage (absence of receipts is itself a signal); the confidentiality of what receipts incidentally capture; the integrity of the schema and the CLI supply chain.

**Adversary classes,** in rough order of how much they should shape the design:

- **A0 — Nobody.** Accident, misconfiguration, a hook that silently stopped firing. By far the most likely cause of a wrong receipt, and the one the design should optimize against first.
- **A1 — The opportunistic developer.** Not malicious; wants the merge gate to stop bothering them. Will disable a hook, retry until green, or reach for the easiest bypass. Defends against nothing sophisticated.
- **A2 — The prompt-injected or misdirected agent.** An agent acting on instructions from a file, issue, or dependency it read. Has write access to the working tree, which as noted below includes the hook configuration.
- **A3 — The malicious insider.** A developer with legitimate repo write access, deliberately falsifying provenance. Controls their own machine completely.
- **A4 — The external attacker with repo write.** Compromised credentials or a malicious contributor. Similar capabilities to A3 but without a durable identity to protect.
- **A5 — The supply-chain attacker.** Compromises the `provene` package itself, or a dependency, to make every downstream receipt lie. The highest-impact and lowest-likelihood adversary — and the one that would end the project.

## 4. Threats

### T-1 — Forged authorship ("the scapegoat")
*A developer writes code by hand, fabricates a receipt attributing it to an agent, signs it with their own identity, commits.*

**Adversary:** A3, A4. **Tier affected:** T1.
**Mitigation:** None, at T1. This is unpreventable by construction and must be stated as such.
**Residual risk — and why it is smaller than it looks:** the attack requires the attacker to volunteer *more* scrutiny onto their own change. Under any sane policy configuration, agent-authored code attracts more review, stricter gates, and higher coverage requirements than human-authored code. The scapegoat attack buys deniability at the price of friction, and the payoff only arrives after something has already broken. It is a real hole with a weak motive.

Partial detection is available and worth building toward: a receipt whose `taskRef` points into a vendor-held session log (a Claude Code session ID, a Copilot `Agent-Logs-Url`) is corroborable after the fact against a system the attacker does not control. That converts forgery from undetectable to detectable-on-audit, which is the standard most change-management controls actually operate at.

### T-2 — Forged verification evidence ("the policy bypass")
*A receipt claims tests ran and passed when they did not, to clear a merge gate.*

**Adversary:** A1, A3. **Tier affected:** T1 only.
**Mitigation: closed.** CI re-runs the tests anyway. When the GitHub Action observes the run and signs the result with the workload identity, the evidence is T2 — produced by a party with no stake in the merge and no access to the developer's machine. A policy that requires T2 for test claims makes forged test evidence useless.
**Residual risk:** low. It reduces to trusting the CI provider, which is the same trust an organization already extends to its build system every time it ships. Worth separating from T-1 when describing what this does: the two are often mentioned in one breath, and only one of them is unsolved.

### T-3 — Concealment (the inverse attack, and the common one)
*Code is agent-authored; someone wants no record of that. The hook is disabled, or the receipt is dropped.*

**Adversary:** A0 (much the most likely), A1, A2, A3.
**Mitigation: conditional on the deployed policy mode. This was previously stated as closed; that was wrong, and v0.2 corrects it.**

A policy requiring a receipt on every commit does turn absence into a blocked merge. But in a real repository the receiptless population is dominated by ordinary causes — dependabot, GitHub web-UI edits, reverts, merges from forks, CI-authored commits, and contributors who never installed the hook. A global requirement therefore has a false-positive rate close to total, and a gate that misfires constantly is a gate that gets exceptions bolted on until it means nothing, or gets switched off.

The defense survives only if exceptions are **declared and version-controlled rather than accumulated informally.** Three policy modes, specified in RFC 0002:

1. **`conditional`** (default) — activates only when the PR carries at least one receipt, then enforces that every agent-attributed path is accounted for. Zero false positives. Defends the integrity of what is claimed. **No defense against concealment at all.**
2. **`required-with-exemptions`** — a receipt is required, with an exemption list that is itself reviewed policy (bot authors, dependabot, reverts, fork merges). The aggregate records how many commits were exempted and under which rule, so erosion shows up in the evidence instead of hiding in a habit. Partial defense, proportional to the discipline of the list.
3. **`enrolled`** — a receipt is required from developers on an enrollment roster and from no one else. A new contributor is never punished; an enrolled developer who disables their hook is detectable. **This is the only mode that closes concealment**, and it needs an org-side roster to work.

**Residual risk:** high under the default mode, low under `enrolled`. **Concealment resistance is a property of the deployed policy mode, not of the product** — see §6.

### T-4 — Hook configuration is inside the agent's blast radius
*Project-level hook configuration for every current agent tool lives in the repository working tree — which is precisely what the agent has write access to. An agent can disable its own receipt emission, whether by instruction, by prompt injection (A2), or by a developer's `sed`.*

**Adversary:** A1, A2, A3. **This is the sharpest practical hole in the v0 design and neither external review raised it.**
**Mitigations, layered:**
1. `provene init` should write hook configuration to **user-level** settings by default, outside the repository and outside the agent's normal write scope. Repo-level config becomes an explicit opt-in with a documented weakening.
2. Any change to hook configuration or to `.provene/` policy files must itself be a flagged event in the receipt covering that commit, and a policy-visible one.
3. T-3's receipt-required gate catches the outcome regardless of the mechanism.
4. `provene doctor` verifies the hook actually fires, so A0 — the silently dead hook — is caught before it becomes a repo that believes it has coverage and does not.

**Residual risk:** moderate, and unavoidable while agent tools keep repo-scoped configuration. Detection, not prevention, is the honest posture.

### T-5 — Receipt/commit binding and replay
*A valid receipt is relocated to a different commit, or reused across branches.*

**Adversary:** A1, A3, A4.
**Mitigation:** the signed payload must bind to specific content — parent commit SHA plus the tree hash or a canonical digest of the changed-file set — never to a branch or a timestamp alone. A receipt that does not name what it covers covers nothing.
**Open design problem for the RFC:** the receipt cannot be stored in-tree and also cover the tree that contains it. Either exclude the receipt path from the covered digest, or attach out-of-tree. Both are viable; pick one in RFC 0001 and make it normative.

### T-6 — Attachment durability: git notes and squash merges
*Not an attack — a correctness failure that behaves like one, and the most likely reason a real repo reports "Provene doesn't work here."*

Git notes live under `refs/notes/*` and are **not pushed or fetched by default**; they require explicit refspec configuration. And most teams squash-merge, which destroys the commits the receipts were bound to — silently converting a fully-evidenced PR into a receiptless merge commit on the default branch.

**Mitigation:** the Action must aggregate the PR's receipts and re-anchor them to the merge commit, producing a PR-level receipt whose covered set is the squashed diff and which references the constituent receipts by digest. This needs to be designed into v0, not discovered in week six. It also interacts with T-5: the re-anchoring step is itself a T2 attestation, which is convenient.

### T-7 — Secret and IP leakage through the receipt
*Receipts record command lines, file paths, and test outcomes. Argv leaks credentials routinely; raw stdout leaks both credentials and source; paths leak internal architecture.*

**Adversary:** anyone who can read the receipt — which, at T2 with Sigstore public signing, may include the **public Rekor transparency log**. Verify carefully what Rekor entries expose before any receipt content reaches them; if the log entry can carry more than an opaque digest, that is a data-exfiltration path built into the default configuration.
**Mitigation:** never store raw stdout — structured results and counts only. Redact argv against a secret-pattern denylist, dropping rather than hashing matches. Make path inclusion configurable. Sign over digests, publish digests.
**Note:** this is a materially larger exposure than prompt plaintext, which is the privacy surface people think of first.

### T-8 — Compromise of the CLI supply chain
*A malicious version of `provene` or one of its dependencies emits plausible false receipts everywhere at once.*

**Adversary:** A5. **Impact: terminal.** A provenance tool that shipped a compromised release does not get a second chance, and it is distributed through npm, which makes this the obvious line of attack and the obvious line of criticism.
**Mitigation:** minimal dependency surface as a hard rule, not an aspiration; publish with npm provenance attestations (dogfooding the entire thesis); pinned lockfiles; reproducible builds as a stated goal; signed releases; a documented disclosure policy from day one.

### T-9 — Trust-root substitution
*A receipt is verified against an attacker-chosen trust root and passes.*

**Adversary:** A3, A4.
**Mitigation:** the receipt records which trust root produced the signature, and the verifier's accepted roots are configuration on the verifier's side, never taken from the receipt. This is the security constraint on the pluggable-signing-backend feature — pluggable at the signer, pinned at the verifier.

### T-10 — Ingest abuse (v1 only)
*Unbounded receipt submission to inflate a bill, exhaust storage, or bury real receipts in noise.*
**Mitigation:** authenticated ingest, per-repo rate limits, content-addressed dedupe. A service that meters repositories rather than receipts has no financial-attack surface here at all, which is a reason to prefer that shape.

### T-11 — Replay of verification evidence across branches
*A change is cherry-picked to another branch. Because binding is by change content (RFC 0001 §4), the receipt travels with it — carrying test evidence gathered against a different base, where the same change might fail.*

**Adversary:** A1, A3; also A0, since this happens accidentally on every backport.
**Note this is not confined to T1.** CI genuinely ran the tests and genuinely signed the result; the receipt is valid at T2 and still describes a run against a base that is not the one now being merged into.
**Mitigation:** each verification run records `baseCommit`, and a policy may require it to equal the pull request's merge base. Stale evidence then fails a policy predicate rather than a signature check — which is the correct layer, since nothing about the signature is wrong.
**Residual risk:** low where the policy predicate is set, and this is a good candidate for a default-on rule rather than an opt-in one.

## 5. Explicit non-goals

State these in the README, not only here. Owning the limitation is worth more than the limitation costs.

Provene does **not**:

- prove that an AI agent authored a change (T-1). It records a signed claim about authorship and, where a vendor session reference exists, makes that claim auditable.
- prevent a malicious insider from fabricating a T1 receipt in an environment they fully control.
- prevent concealment, except under the `enrolled` policy mode. Under the default mode a disabled hook produces a PR the gate does not inspect.
- verify code quality, correctness, or security. It records what verification ran and what the result was.
- detect AI-generated code. It is not a classifier, and it makes no probabilistic judgments.
- replace human review. It tells a reviewer what was and was not verified.

## 6. Claim discipline

Drop-in language. Every public claim should be phrasable in this frame; if it cannot be, it is overclaiming.

> **A Provene receipt is a cryptographically signed record of what an agent session claimed to do and what verification was observed to run.** Receipts generated locally are self-attestations by the developer's environment — designed for visibility, attribution, and compliance tracking, not for defending against a malicious insider. Receipts counter-signed in CI carry independently observed verification evidence, and policies can require them.

**On concealment, name the mode or say nothing.** "Provene detects when an agent's involvement is hidden" is true only under `enrolled` enforcement. Under the default `conditional` mode it is false. Any claim about concealment MUST carry the mode it assumes.

Three sentences that should never appear in any Provene material: *"you can't hide that an agent wrote this"* without naming the enforcement mode; *"proves that Claude wrote this code"* and *"prevents developers from bypassing test requirements"* without the CI qualifier. Use **records**, **attests**, **observed**, **verified in CI**. Never **proves**, unqualified.

**The whole position in one paragraph**, for anyone who needs to summarise it:

> Receipts are self-attestations at T1 and forgeable by their signer. Tiering is the mitigation: CI counter-signature (T2) makes verification evidence independently observed, which closes forged test evidence. Forged *authorship* is not preventable without sitting in the inference path, and is an explicit non-goal rather than something defended against. Transparency-log timestamping and vendor session references give after-the-fact auditability.

## 7. What this document decided for the schema

These were settled here before RFC 0001 was written, and each one is now in it:

1. **`attestation.tier`** — T0/T1/T2/T3 enum, with the attester identity and the trust root recorded alongside it. Load-bearing for every policy rule.
2. **`covers`** — explicit binding to parent commit plus a canonical digest of the changed-file set, with the receipt path itself excluded from that set (T-5).
3. **Per-file verification evidence**, not just an aggregate, so path-scoped policies are expressible at all.
4. **A redaction contract** — which fields may contain free text, what is dropped versus digested, and what is safe to publish to a transparency log (T-7).
5. **`taskRef`** as a vendor-held session URI, distinct from and more important than the task digest.
6. **PR-level aggregate receipts**, so squash merges do not destroy the chain (T-6).
7. **Canonical serialization** (JCS/RFC 8785) with normative field-ordering and unknown-field rules — because a signature is over bytes.

## 8. Questions this document opened

**Answered.**

*Does a Rekor entry expose anything beyond an opaque digest?* Yes. Fulcio embeds the signer's OIDC identity in the certificate, and that certificate is published to the transparency log even when the identity is not otherwise public. A private repository publishing to the public log therefore leaks its contributors and the timing of their activity. This is why the emitter never publishes from a private repository, and why GitHub selects the Sigstore instance by repository visibility rather than the signer choosing (RFC 0001 §11). T-7 was upgraded accordingly.

*Local T1 signing, or unsigned T0 promoted in CI?* T0 promoted in CI. T1 remains specified and unimplemented. A developer sees no signature until CI runs, which is a worse demo and a more honest one: nothing on the developer's machine is in a position to attest anything a policy should accept.

**Still open.**

*Should `provene verify` refuse T1 receipts by default, requiring an explicit `--accept-self-attested`?* It currently reports the tier and says, in as many words, that a self-attestation satisfies no policy on its own. That is weaker than refusing. Defaults are the strongest statement this project will make about how it expects to be used, and this one has not been argued out.

*What does a second emitter need?* The format is not specific to Claude Code, but only Claude Code has an emitter, so nothing has yet tested that claim. The first emitter for another agent will find the assumptions this one hid.
